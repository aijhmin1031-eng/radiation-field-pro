Example: an N-80 reference field for a survey-meter energy-response test

Field Pro · updated 28 October 2026

An energy-response test of an H*(10) dose-rate meter needs reference beams whose spectrum is known well enough to name the conventional quantity value at the test point. N-80, from the narrow-spectrum series of ISO 4037-1, is one of the qualities such tests step through. This example sets it up, checks it against the table and turns a chamber reading into the value the meter under test should show.

Open the workbench with the N-80 preset; these are also the defaults.

From chamber reading to conventional value

Suppose the reference chamber, after its own calibration factor and kρ, gives an air kerma rate of 1.00 mGy/h at the test point. The conventional ambient dose equivalent rate the meter should indicate is 1.00 × 1.723 = 1.72 mSv/h. The meter's relative response at this quality is its indication divided by 1.72 mSv/h, and that ratio is what the type-test limits apply to. The IEC 60846-1 summary on RadiMeter lists those requirements.

What the numbers say

HVL is the sensitive check; the conversion coefficient is not. Moving the tube voltage by 2 kV in the workbench shifts HVL1 from 0.5753 to 0.5555 mm Cu at 78 kV (−3.4%) and to 0.6008 at 82 kV (+4.4%). A 0.1 mm error in the copper moves it by about 1.5% either way. Across all four of those changes H*(10)/Ka stays between 1.722 and 1.724 Sv/Gy. A measured HVL1 outside the band therefore tells you the beam is not N-80 as specified, usually through kilovoltage or filtration, long before the dose conversion itself is in doubt.

Average over the spectrum. Reading the monoenergetic table at Ē = 64.96 keV would give 1.735 for H*(10)/Ka and 1.893 for Hp(10, 0°)/Ka, 0.7% and 0.9% above the spectrum averages. In a test whose limits are tens of percent that is small, but it is a systematic error with a known sign, and there is no reason to carry it.

Output costs filtration. The 2 mm of copper lets through 1.51% of the air kerma that the inherent filtration alone would deliver, about a factor of 66. Plan tube current and distance with that in mind before booking beam time; the kerma overlay on the spectrum chart shows that 80% of what remains lies between 55 and 75 keV, peaking at 67 keV.

Angle matters for personal dosemeters. For a personal dosemeter tested on the slab at 60°, the conversion coefficient drops to 1.496 Sv/Gy, 20% below normal incidence. Set α in panel 02 to the angle of each irradiation rather than correcting by hand.

The same steps for a nuclide field are in the Cs-137 example, and the Guide explains each panel.
