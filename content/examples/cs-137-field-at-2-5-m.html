A caesium-137 irradiator is the workhorse of a radiation-protection calibration room: one line at 661.657 keV, a half-life long enough to plan around and short enough to matter. This example takes a source certified ten years ago, brings its activity to today, and gives the air kerma rate, the dose-equivalent rates and the lead half-value layer at a 2.5 m test point.

What the numbers say

Dates are part of the calibration. Over 10.82 years the source has lost 22% of its activity, and it keeps losing 2.3% a year. A rate table written at commissioning and never re-dated is 2.3% wrong after one year and 11% wrong after five. Entering the evaluation date each time costs nothing.

The rate scales with the inverse square of distance, and only that. At 1 m the same source gives 590.8 µGy/h, 6.25 times the 2.5 m value. The point-source formula ignores the air in between; with the engine's own attenuation data, 2.5 m of air would remove 2.3% of the primary kerma, while scatter from the air, the collimator and the room adds some back. Those effects are why a laboratory fixes its rates by measurement with a calibrated chamber and uses a calculation like this one to plan and to check.

The calculated rate constant sits slightly low. Dividing the rate by activity and multiplying by d² gives 0.0758 µGy·m²·MBq⁻¹·h⁻¹, 1.7% below the literature value of 0.0771 used in the test suite. The engine models only the gamma line; the barium K X-rays near 32 keV are absent, and with them a small low-energy share of the kerma.

Lead attenuators. In the narrow-beam model each 5.6 mm of lead halves the air kerma rate, and 22.4 mm (four half-value layers) leaves 6.25%, about 5.9 µGy/h at 2.5 m. Real attenuators in a collimated beam add forward scatter, so the measured reduction is smaller; the narrow-beam value is the right one for describing beam quality and the wrong one for shielding a room.

Choose the quantity before the coefficient. At this energy Hp(10) on the slab exceeds H*(10) by only 1.1%, so mixing them up hides easily; it is still a different quantity with a different phantom, and a personal dosemeter must be compared with Hp(10).
